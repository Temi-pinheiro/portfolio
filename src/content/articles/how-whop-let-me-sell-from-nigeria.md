I've been in tech for a long time, and I've been around enough to have tried to set up something of my own: to sell a service or a product online the way I wanted to. Not through a third party like Shopify or Etsy, but the way the people I followed did, with Lemon Squeezy or Stripe. For one reason or another (usually my location), I just could never do it. Then one day, through YouTube, I found Whop, and all that changed. This is what it took, what it costs, and who it's for.

## Before Whop, how did I get paid for anything digital?

Honestly, I never had. Before Whop, I had never been able to get paid for anything digital. The closest I got was finding out about Stripe Atlas, which lets you register a US company online (which would finally give me access to Stripe, amongst other things). But I didn't have the capital to register a company, and I needed to get paid for my services directly from my website, today.

So getting paid meant hoping people would make a transfer: to my Nigerian account, or to the Revolut or Grey accounts I held. I always felt that was unprofessional, and that it could even put some people off. Some services you want paid for at a checkout, not after a conversation. For a very long time there was nothing that let me take money online the way I needed, until I found Whop.

## Why couldn't I just use Stripe?

**Stripe**, and even **Lemon Squeezy**, made it really difficult to accept money in Nigeria. Especially Stripe: I couldn't even register as a business, and I tried multiple times.

I used to build front end applications for clients all around the world, and one time I worked for a client in the UK, and the backend engineer and I were both in Nigeria. He was trying to set up the Stripe account so he could keep building the API, and I was waiting on him because there was a payment integration we had to do. We were blocked for the longest time, because we just couldn't do it ourselves. We had to wait for the CEO to come back and register it himself. It's not something you can do with any details you have from Nigeria: not your phone number, not your tax ID, not your business registration number. We were simply blocked.

With **Lemon Squeezy,** honestly, to this day I don't know if it's possible to register as a Nigerian. The process felt convoluted, and it wasn't easy to get through. I also tried **Commas**, but I wasn't sure it would offer exactly what I needed, because again, it came off as very American. I've had so many bad experiences with anything that did, or anything that even seemed Western. A lot of them just don't support Nigeria, and I honestly can't begin to understand why.

## What about Paystack or Flutterwave?

I would have considered them, but they didn't offer the reach I needed. These would have been easy to integrate if all the customers I wanted were Nigerian. But whether it was [the audit service](https://whop.com/temipinheiro/) I sold at the time, [the agent builds](/mariposa) I sell now, [the guide](/quiet-edge) I created, or [Holo'd](https://holod-drab.vercel.app/), the game I made with a payment layer, I needed anybody from anywhere who came across it to be able to pay without having to reach out to me personally.

I wanted everything to be as streamlined as possible, and the only thing that offered me the whole package was Whop.

## How did you find Whop, and when did it click?

I found Whop through a YouTube challenge I joined about a year ago. At that point I didn't know what it was. The person running the channel turned out to be a part owner of Whop, so they presented it for what it was, and I started to understand it: a platform where you could sell things. What I was watching was a course on how to sell digital products, and I got intrigued by the idea that maybe I could sell digital products one day.

But I honestly didn't use Whop for about a year, because I didn't have anything to sell. I kept it in the back of my mind: if I ever wanted to sell anything online, Whop was something I could use. That was before I even knew how well it supported sellers in Nigeria.

The first place I tried it was [Holo'd](https://holod-drab.vercel.app/), the gacha game I made. It let me put payments directly into my app, the way I'd seen so many other people do in theirs. It worked so well and so seamlessly that I was honestly in awe. It clicked instantly: this was exactly what I'd been looking for, and it was what I was going to keep using.

## What did setting it up involve?

That's the surprising part. Setting up the account was so quick. I went to register and found I already had an account from that first time I participated in a challenge, and that was all I needed to make a business.

At the time I didn't have a registered company I wanted to use for these projects, so I wanted to see if I could do it as just me, an individual. I went through the verification, and it took 10 minutes. I honestly didn't believe it. There was no way that was all I needed to do, but it was.

## How did I wire it into my code?

Whop has, as far as I can tell, a world-class MCP. Once I connected it to Claude Code and created the right API keys, everything moved quickly. It's one of my favourite things about Whop, and probably one of the best MCPs I've used. Once the account was verified, the rest was pretty much all done through the MCP, and it was seamless.

I also like how many ways Whop gives you to do things. On Holo'd, checkout isn't on the page itself. You buy a pack and it takes you to a checkout page, a custom one with the details you give it. When you've paid, it brings you right back, shows you what you bought, and you're good. On [the Quiet Edge page](/quiet-edge), checkout is built into my website, so you never leave. You pay on my site and you get your guide.

That might not seem like much, but having those two options changes how it's presented, and that matters a lot to me as a front-end-leaning developer. It means I decide the experience my user has. That freedom is really important to me.

The other thing I love is how many local currencies it supports. A boy from Nigeria can have customers all the way in Brazil. That magnifies my reach and makes me, in a sense, limitless in the audience I can reach. I think that's fantastic.

## What does a sale actually leave you?

I think the fees are pretty reasonable. Obviously I wish there weren't any, but for the enablement they give, I'm happy with it.

The first real sale of The Quiet Edge came from a buyer here in Nigeria. They paid ₦37,062 by Nigerian bank transfer for the $27 guide, and the checkout showed them the price in naira. Whop took $1.69 in fees, which left me $25.31. Whop also holds back 10.5% of that sale, $2.66, as a reserve until February.

Small prices are a different story. A $1 pack on Holo'd leaves me between 54 and 59 cents, because there's a fixed 30-cent fee on every payment on top of the percentage. On a guide that's nothing. On a $1 pack it's almost half.

For reference, Whop's [published rates](https://docs.whop.com/payments-and-billing/fees/regional-fees) when I checked in November 2026: Nigerian bank transfers and OPay cost 3.9% plus 30 cents, and a card adds 1.5% when the buyer is outside the US.

## How do you get the money out?

I've made a withdrawal, and Whop gives you multiple options, bank included, and in multiple currencies. For someone like me with a multi-currency account, it's really useful to withdraw in the currency I actually want to spend in.

The problem I did have is that, depending on the payout method, you can wait on the transfer. The fastest option, I think, is crypto. You verify a crypto wallet, which took me under 12 hours, and then you can withdraw instantly. Honestly, it was a breeze, and I'll probably keep using it because it's the quickest.

I'm really pleased with how flexible withdrawals are. It means I'll probably never get stuck trying to get money out of Whop.

## What surprised me, good or bad?

The good surprise: Whop Websites. It lets you put a product site on your own domain and sell from it, and it comes free. If you need a funnel of some sort with your own branding or name on it, I think that's a wonderful thing to have.

The bad: there were still some kinks to work out. When I was first setting up payments by bank transfer within Nigeria, the transfer flow was kind of broken. It takes you off the page and doesn't bring you back automatically, so you have to go back to the page yourself. When I tested with my Nigerian card, it worked perfectly.

So I know I've given buyers the flexibility to pay however they prefer, and there are local payment methods in almost every country. It's not perfect, but it's the closest thing to perfect I've been able to use.

I also think getting to payouts/withdrawals could be quicker, there are a lot of things on the dashboard that look like withdrawals but aren't.

## What did Whop unlock for me?

It isn't so much what it let me ship as the possibilities it opened up. Before Whop, I never really had any prospect of selling my own products to people abroad. I was blocked everywhere, either by some registration I couldn't do because of where I live, or by where my business is registered.

That was stifling for a while, especially mentally. I stopped thinking about what to build and sell, because what was the point? Whop opened up a world of possibilities for me. That's the biggest unlock I got from it: more the mental shift in what felt possible than the things I've actually built and shipped with it.

## Who is Whop wrong for?

If you're in Nigeria (I can't speak for the whole of Africa) and your sales are purely local, I don't think you need Whop. It might just be overhead, since you can get a Paystack, Moniepoint or Flutterwave account. The local solutions work really well for a local context. But once there's any international side to what you sell, Whop helps you break through a lot of barriers, both in getting paid and in withdrawing to pay yourself.

## What would I tell another Nigerian builder about to launch something paid?

Really look at your possible audience and your ICP. Even if your audience is Nigerian, could it be a Nigerian living abroad who doesn't have a Nigerian account? Could it be someone who isn't Nigerian at all, maybe another African? I can't speak to every local payment option out there. But having used Whop, once anything I do reaches beyond my local scope, it's the first thing I think of. For reach and accessibility, I think it's the ultimate solution.

In all fairness, read up on their pages and see everything they do. Look at their percentages. If it's not an amount you're comfortable with, I'd definitely tell you to find someone else. But for ease of access it feels plug and play, and for me Whop is a no-brainer.

## What's next for you on the money side?

For now it's mostly private, but there's a lot in the pipeline with partners here and abroad, and Whop might make a bunch of things possible for those projects too.
